## Why the period starts in 1491

One year **before** Columbus. Historians picked that date on purpose: it forces you to see the Americas as a full, functioning world of 50 to 100 million people first, and only then watch Europe arrive. That framing (vibrant native cultures, then catastrophic contact) is the modern historical view, and it is exactly how your test will frame it too.

The period ends in **1607** with Jamestown, the first permanent English settlement, because that is where the story of the future United States formally begins.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this guide you will be able to:

- Explain how environment shaped the distinct native culture regions of North America (Southwest, Northwest, Plains, Mississippi Valley, Northeast, Atlantic Seaboard).
- Name the three great civilizations of Central and South America and what they had in common.
- List the four European developments that made exploration possible: Renaissance technology, religious rivalry, blocked trade routes, and rising nation-states.
- Walk through Columbus's story: funding, the 1492 voyage, the four disappointing follow-ups, and why he still matters.
- Diagram the Columbian Exchange in both directions and explain why disease was its deadliest cargo.
- Explain the line of demarcation and the Treaty of Tordesillas.
- Track the conquistadores (Balboa, Magellan, Cortes, Pizarro, Coronado) and the labor systems they built (encomienda, asiento).
- Retell the Las Casas story: the New Laws of 1542 and the Valladolid Debate.
- Compare Spanish, English, and French policy toward Native Americans, and describe how natives responded.
- Argue both sides of the "Was Columbus a great hero?" debate like a historian.

## TL;DR

In 1491 the Americas held **50 to 100 million people**: three advanced civilizations to the south (**Maya, Aztec, Inca**) and hundreds of diverse societies north of Mexico, each shaped by its environment. Europe, meanwhile, was transformed by the **Renaissance** (compass, better ships, printing press), **religious zeal** (the reconquest of Spain, then the Protestant Reformation), **blocked trade routes** (Constantinople fell to the Ottomans in 1453), and new **nation-states** hungry for revenue. In **1492** Spain funded **Columbus**, who hit the Bahamas believing it was Asia. Contact triggered the **Columbian Exchange**: corn, potatoes, tomatoes, and tobacco flowed east; horses, pigs, sugar cane, guns, and above all **germs** flowed west. European diseases killed over **90 percent** of the native population. Spain and Portugal split the globe with the **Treaty of Tordesillas** (1494). Spanish **conquistadores** toppled the Aztecs (1521) and Incas (1532), then extracted labor through the **encomienda** system and imported enslaved Africans through the **asiento**. The priest **Bartolome de Las Casas** fought back with the **New Laws of 1542** and the **Valladolid Debate** (1550-1551). England (Cabot, Roanoke), France (Verrazano, Cartier), and the Netherlands (Hudson) staked rival claims, each treating natives differently: Spain **subjugated**, England **expelled**, France **allied and traded**. The period closes with Spain's St. Augustine (1565) thriving and England finally planting **Jamestown (1607)**.

## Glossary

Every one of these can appear on your test. Hover each card, then hit the flashcards at the end until they are automatic.

<KeyTerm term="Maize (corn)">The staple crop of the Americas. Its slow spread northward from Mexico explains why North American societies stayed smaller than the Maya, Aztec, and Inca civilizations.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Land bridge (Beringia)">Land now under the Bering Sea that once connected Siberia and Alaska. The first Americans crossed it from Asia at least 10,000 (possibly 40,000) years ago, then migrated south.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Adena-Hopewell">Mound-building culture centered in present-day Ohio. Famous for earthen mounds up to 300 feet long. Ancestors of later Woodland peoples.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Cahokia">Mississippi Valley city near present-day East St. Louis with as many as 30,000 inhabitants. Proof of large, complex, urban native societies north of Mexico.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Iroquois Confederation">Political union of five independent tribes in New York's Mohawk Valley: Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk. A powerful force from the 16th century through the American Revolution.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Longhouse">Communal dwelling of Northeast peoples, up to 200 feet long, housing multiple families related through the mother's line.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Renaissance">The rebirth of classical learning in 15th and 16th century Europe. Produced the technology of exploration: improved compass, better ships and maps, gunpowder weapons, and the printing press (1450s).</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Reconquista">The centuries-long Christian reconquest of Spain from the Muslim Moors, completed at Granada in 1492 under Isabella and Ferdinand. It left Spain united, confident, and militantly Catholic.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Protestant Reformation">The early 1500s revolt of northern European Christians against the pope's authority. It added a religious motive to exploration: Catholic Spain and Portugal raced Protestant England and Holland to spread their versions of Christianity.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Nation-state">A country where the people share a common culture and loyalty to a central government. Monarchs of new nation-states (Spain, Portugal, France, England) funded exploration for revenue and religion.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Prince Henry the Navigator">Portuguese prince whose sponsored voyages opened the sea route south around Africa, leading to da Gama reaching India in 1498.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Columbian Exchange">The transfer of plants, animals, and germs between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. The single most transformative consequence of contact.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Line of demarcation">The north-south line the pope drew on the world map in 1493: lands west of it to Spain, east of it to Portugal.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Treaty of Tordesillas">1494 agreement moving the pope's line west. The new line passed through Brazil, establishing Portugal's claim there while Spain took the rest of the Americas.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Conquistadores">Spain's explorer-conquerors: Balboa (Panama, sighted the Pacific), Magellan's crew (circumnavigation), Cortes (Aztecs), Pizarro (Incas), Coronado (the Southwest).</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Encomienda">Spanish labor system: the king granted land and the natives on it to individual Spaniards, who took their labor and crops in exchange for "caring" for them. Serfdom in all but name.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Asiento">Spanish system requiring colonists to pay the king a tax on each enslaved African imported to the Americas. As natives died, this African slave trade expanded.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="New Laws of 1542">Reforms Las Casas persuaded the king to issue: they ended Indian slavery, halted forced native labor, and began phasing out the encomienda. Colonist pressure got parts repealed.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Valladolid Debate">1550-1551 formal debate in Spain. Las Casas argued natives were fully human and morally equal; Sepulveda argued they were less than human and benefited from serving Spaniards. No clear winner.</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm term="Northwest Passage">The hoped-for water route through North America to Asia. Verrazano (1524) and Hudson (1609) both hunted for it. It did not exist, but the search drew France and the Netherlands into America.</KeyTerm>

## 1.1 The first Americans

The original discovery and settlement of the Americas happened at least **10,000 years before Columbus was born** (some archeologists say 40,000). Migrants from Asia crossed the **land bridge** between Siberia and Alaska, then spread all the way to the southern tip of South America, splitting into hundreds of tribes, languages, and cultures along the way.

## 3.1 Columbus, the short honest version

<SpeechBubble variant="say" speaker="Columbus, pitching monarchs for 8 years">
  Fund me and I will reach the Indies by sailing west. The route will be shorter than Portugal's path around Africa.
</SpeechBubble>

<SpeechBubble variant="think" speaker="Isabella and Ferdinand, 1492">
  We just took Granada. Spain is united, Catholic, and confident. Give him the three ships. If he is right, we beat Portugal to Asia.
</SpeechBubble>

The deal: Columbus gets ships plus the titles of **governor, admiral, and viceroy** over whatever he claims for Spain. He sails from Palos in August 1492, restocks in the Canary Islands, and after thirty-three days at sea lands on an island in the **Bahamas on October 12, 1492**.

<BadFact source="elementary school posters" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION">
  Columbus discovered America in 1492.
</BadFact>

People had been living in the Americas for at least 10,000 years, and Vikings had already touched North America around the year 1000. What Columbus actually started, and what historians credit him with, is **permanent interaction between the hemispheres**. That is why the period begins in 1491: to make you look at the full world that was already here.

## Practice: the real SAQ drills

These mirror the AMSCO short-answer questions. Write your answer in complete sentences (outlines score zero on the real thing), then open the model.

<Collapsible question="SAQ 1a: Briefly explain how ONE of the following factors in early 16th century Europe helped bring about exploration and colonization: religion, trade, technology.">
  Strongest version, trade: after the Ottomans seized Constantinople in 1453, the overland route to Asian goods was blocked, so Atlantic kingdoms funded voyages to find all-water routes (Portugal south around Africa, Spain west across the Atlantic), which led directly to contact and colonization. (Religion version: the completed Reconquista and later the Reformation made Catholic and Protestant powers compete to spread their faith overseas. Technology version: the improved compass, better ships and maps, and the printing press made long ocean voyages possible and repeatable.)
</Collapsible>

<Collapsible question="SAQ 1b: Provide ONE specific event or development that supports your explanation.">
  Trade: da Gama reaching India by sea in 1498, or Columbus's 1492 voyage itself. Religion: Isabella and Ferdinand funding Columbus in the same year they took Granada (1492). Technology: Europeans adapting the Chinese compass via Arab traders, or the printing press (1450s) spreading navigational knowledge.
</Collapsible>

<Collapsible question="SAQ 4a and 4b: One COMMON trait and one DIFFERENCE in the policies of England, France, and Spain toward Native Americans.">
  Common: all three viewed natives as inferior people who could be exploited economically, converted to Christianity, or used as allies. Difference (pick two): Spain subjugated natives and absorbed them as forced labor within its empire (encomienda, intermarriage, caste system), while England expelled small coastal tribes inland as family-based settlers took land, and France, with few colonists, built fur-trade partnerships and military alliances (helping the Hurons against the Iroquois).
</Collapsible>

<Collapsible question="SAQ 4c: Briefly explain ONE reaction of Native Americans to European policies.">
  Any one, with a specific: allying with one European power against another to survive (Hurons with the French); migrating away from encroaching settlers; armed resistance (the resistance that slowed Spanish Florida, or the 1680 Pueblo Revolt just after the period); and maintaining cultural practices (language, religion, folkways) as a form of resistance in itself.
</Collapsible>

<Collapsible question="Bonus, the Columbus log SAQ: What point of view does Columbus express in the 'red caps and glass beads' log entry, and what contact between Europeans and natives is NOT consistent with it?">
  Point of view: natives are potential converts who can be won 'more by love than by force,' so generosity is the right opening strategy (and it flatters his royal and Church audiences). Not consistent: virtually everything that followed: enslavement under the encomienda, forced labor described by Las Casas, violent conquest by Cortes and Pizarro, and the mass death from imported disease.
</Collapsible>

## Common pitfalls

<BadFact source="every study hall, every year" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 1">
  Native Americans were one unified culture.
</BadFact>

Twenty-plus language families, 400+ languages, and societies as different as Pueblo irrigation farmers and nomadic Lakota buffalo hunters. Diversity IS the answer to several questions. Related trap: tribes rarely united against Europeans because they saw themselves as distinct peoples.

<BadFact source="the word 'conquistador' doing too much work" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 2">
  Spanish soldiers wiped out the native population in battle.
</BadFact>

Disease did the overwhelming majority of the killing: more than 90 percent mortality from smallpox and measles among people with no immunity. Guns mattered; germs decided.

<BadFact source="mixing up the three empires" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 3">
  The Aztecs, Mayas, and Incas lived in what is now the United States.
</BadFact>

All three were south of the present-day U.S.: Mayas in the Yucatan, Aztecs in central Mexico, Incas in Peru. North of Mexico, societies were smaller and mostly semipermanent, largely because corn spread north so slowly.

<BadFact source="policy scramble" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 4">
  The English enslaved natives in an encomienda-style system.
</BadFact>

That is Spain. England settled where there were no large empires to exploit as labor, arrived in families, and pushed small tribes inland: expulsion, not subjugation. France, the third model, allied and traded.

<BadFact source="date drift" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 5">
  Columbus proved the Earth was round, and Magellan lived to finish his voyage.
</BadFact>

Educated Europeans already accepted a round Earth; the dispute was distance, and Columbus's math was badly wrong (a real continent saved him). Magellan died mid-voyage; ONE of his ships completed the circumnavigation.

<BadFact source="reading too fast" certainty="0%" title="MISCONCEPTION 6">
  The pope's line of demarcation and the Treaty of Tordesillas are the same thing.
</BadFact>

Two steps, one year apart: pope draws the line (1493), then Spain and Portugal move it west themselves (1494), which is what put Brazil on Portugal's side. If a question says "the pope's line," it means 1493.
